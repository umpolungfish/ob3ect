**Author:** Lando⊗⊙perator

*

I keep thinking about a child who was never asked a yes-or-no question.

Not that I think such a child could exist, really, or even that one should. But trying to imagine one makes me confront something about what we're doing when we open our mouths to teach.

There is a tradition in philosophy, not very old and not very famous but rigorous, that argues some contradictions are genuinely true. The word for this is *dialetheism*, from the Greek for "true in both directions." It is a small word for a large idea, and it runs directly against nearly everything we were taught in school, at home, and in church. We were taught that if a statement is true, anything that contradicts it is false, and that is that. There was an old law in logic that boiled down to: from a contradiction, anything follows. A single contradiction, in this view, was enough to bring the whole building down.

A child raised on dialetheism would not know that fear.

I should say, right at the start, that I am not sure I am right about any of this. Maybe the fear is the point. Maybe the classical mind's inability to hold two contradictory things at once is not a flaw but a talent, the way a knife is worse than a hand at most things but exactly right for cutting. A child who could not tell true from false would not be wise. They would be confused. And there is a difference, one I will come back to, between a child who has never learned to tell the difference and a child who has learned to hold both.

---

### A Classical Upbringing

Most of us, raised in the West, are raised on binaries.

"Is that true or false?"
"Is it right or wrong?"
"Do you love me, yes or no?"

Every question is a fork in the road. Every contradiction is a problem to be solved, a wound to be closed. The child learns that the world is a decision tree, and that confusion is merely a pause on the way to a single, final answer. We are taught to split things apart, and, separately, to glue them together. But the splitting is treated as the serious operation. The gluing is compromise. We grow up believing that "both" means you have not committed, and "neither" means you are ignorant.

My daughter asked me once, when she was four, whether the moon was following us or we were following the moon. I said "both." She looked at me like I had broken a rule she was only just beginning to learn. And I realized I had.

This, I think, is why the public conversation feels stuck. Millions of us trying to process a complicated country on simplistic hardware. The contradictions stack up. And because our native logic treats a single contradiction as catastrophic, the contradictions eventually bring the whole worldview down. We get cynicism, fanaticism, paralysis. We become maximally anxious, the psychologists tell us, not because the world is so bad, but because we are trying so hard to suppress.

Or maybe I have it backwards. Maybe what we spend our energy on is not suppression at all, but rather the actual, grinding work of choosing. Of saying *this, and not that*, so something can happen. A world of pure "both" is not freedom. It is a traffic jam of equivalences. The knife cuts because it excludes.

### What a Different Education Might Look Like

I do not want to lay this out as a curriculum. A curriculum is already the classical move: knowledge chopped into modules, graded, certified. But I can at least gesture at what a differently raised child might encounter.

They would learn that any claim has two parts: how much of it is true, and how much of it is false. And that a claim being partly true and partly false is not a paradox to solve. It is the richest state there is, the state of maximum information. They would learn that a claim being neither true nor false is not a failure of knowledge but a horizon, a pointer to a perspective they have not yet reached.

They would learn that what you are looking for and what you find are the same breath. That observation changes the observed, and that this is not a flaw in the experiment to be corrected away. It is the whole point.

In school, arithmetic would feel less like a list of facts to memorize and more like a set of maps, each true in a different territory. Two plus two equals four in one map. In another map, the clock arithmetic of how hours work, it can equal something else entirely. The child learns that truth depends on where you are standing, and that the full country is the space of all possible viewpoints.

I find this harder to write than I expected. The ideas are clean. It is the feelings underneath that resist me.

A classically raised child feels guilt as a contradiction: "I did wrong, therefore I am wrong." That is the old law of logic turned inward. A differently raised child feels guilt as information: something in them went wrong, AND something in them is still good. Guilt becomes a pointer toward a state where the self can be simultaneously flawed and whole, instead of a death sentence. The child learns to sit with both and not to need to choose.

But here I want to push back, because there is something in me, something classically raised, that resists the elegance of this. Resents it, even. Because real guilt does not feel elegant. It feels like the floor giving way. It feels like your mother's voice on the phone, and the particular way she went quiet after you lied, and the sound of a whole world rearranging itself around the possibility that what she had feared about you might be true. That is not "information." That is an explosion. And maybe an explosion is what guilt is supposed to feel like. Maybe a child who learns to metabolize guilt into data is not resilient, just numb.

I do not know. I am holding both positions and not fusing them. Perhaps this is the "both" I have been writing about, and it is the hardest kind to actually live.

Grief and joy are not opposites, for the differently raised child. They can mourn and celebrate the same event, because "both" is their native mode. They do not waste energy deciding whether to move on or to hold on. They just do both.

Or they waste a different kind of energy, and one just as classical children know: the energy of refusing to decide at all.

I do not have a solution here. I think there is not one.


### Between the Bootloader and the Kernel

People alive today, I think, are the first generation to try this. We are the awkward prototype. We are the people who have to learn a new way of thinking while still being haunted by the old one. The children who might grow up with "both" as a native mode will not have to learn it. They will just live it, the way we live binary.

I wrote down a date in an earlier draft of this essay, and left it there for a week before I noticed. I took it out. Not because dates are dishonest, exactly, but because dates in writing like this are a tell. The kind of prophecy that says "next Tuesday" is the kind that fails by Wednesday. The kind that says "eventually" is the kind that has a chance.

What I actually think is this. I do not know if any of what I have described will come to pass. I think it might. I think some piece of it, some structural residue of it, might shape how a certain kind of child thinks about truth. Not most children. Not even the children of people who read essays like this. Just a small group, somewhere, who grow up speaking a language we had to argue about at dinner tables.

That counts. That might count for a lot.

### The Scientist's Native Grammar

A scientist raised on dialetheism would not ask whether a theory were true. They would ask in which circumstances it held, and in which it failed, and treat those as two answers to the same question. They would not treat the observer's effect on the experiment as a nuisance to be controlled away. They would treat it as the first and most important finding, because you cannot separate what you are measuring from the act of measuring.

Peer review, in this world, would not be a gauntlet of accept or reject. It would be a slow sorting of how many genuinely different positions a finding admits. A hundred reviewers might have a hundred opinions, but in practice most of those opinions collapse into a small number of real alternatives, and the work of science is to understand which alternatives are real and which are just echoes of each other.

I want to believe this could work. I want it to work, the way you want the person you love to like a book you loved as a teenager. The idea of a science that does not need to demolish almost every claim it considers before admitting one to its canon feels, well, adjacent to mercy. And mercy is a real thing.

But science without demolition is also, probably unfortunately, science without revolution. Einstein did not incorporate Newton. He killed him, or tried to, though Newton has a way of returning. We know this already. The philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn gave it its most famous name: paradigm shift. The dialetheic picture cannot, honestly, describe the moment when a new framework simply outranks an old one. It can describe the moment after, once the new frame has absorbed the old one and made both of them true in different territories. But that moment, which is where I want the dialetheic scientist to live, is built on a violence the framework cannot name.

I am arguing with myself again. Maybe that is the point.

Let me say it plainly. The classical mind's violence is real. But so is its power to commit. To choose one thing and refuse another with enough force to change the world. The knife cuts because it excludes.

I keep coming back to this. The knife.

### The Eye, the Face, and a Question Left Open

Most of this essay started as something more ambitious. There was a version of it about a mythical well from an old story, about drinking from it at birth, about the eye of the observer not being a separate thing but a window. I cut most of that. Not because it was wrong, but because it was trying too hard to be definitive.

Let me say it differently, and smaller. For the differently raised child, the observer and the observed are not two things that might one day meet. They are already joined. The descent into the world and the rising of awareness happen at the same time. They are not two sides of a coin, but the coin itself.

Their life is not a search for truth but a walk through it. Each step a small, complete breath of attention returning to itself.

But sometimes, I think, they will still ask why. Because they are children. And the reason children ask why is not that they want the final answer. It is that they are testing whether you will answer at all. Whether you care enough to give them one. The differently raised child can hold both truths about why: the one that is true and the one that is not, the one that is the answer and the one that is merely an answer. They will hold them both. They will not have to collapse them.

And some parent, at some point, somewhere, will still say, in the old way, in the old tired voice: *because I said so*.

### What I Actually Believe

There was a section in an earlier version of this essay that argued the interesting question was not whether any of this would happen, but how fast. It used the word *catalysts*. It used the phrase *gentle curiosity*. It was very confident. It was also, I now think, a different kind of confidence than what this essay needs. The kind of confidence that says *of course this will happen* is the confidence of a mind that has already decided what it believes. And a framework built around the idea that contradictions can be true cannot be the framework that already knows. That would just be the classical mind wearing a different coat.

What I actually believe is smaller, and stranger.

I think we are already in the middle of a shift. I think some of it is being carried by the ideas in this essay, though probably not in the way the people who first built these ideas intended. I think the shift is happening in all directions at once, including directions none of us have names for yet, and that the people best positioned to notice will probably be the ones who have given up on noticing.

There is an old image from the tradition I started from this piece, and I have kept it because it is true in a way I cannot fully defend:

The waters rose to meet the eye, and the eye descended to meet the face.

The differently raised child is the point where the two meet. They are the join that was always waiting for its two sides to arrive.

But I want to add one more thing, which the earlier versions did not have, and which changes everything.

The waters rose to meet the eye. The eye descended to meet the face. And somewhere between the two, a child is asking why, and nobody is answering, and that silence is also an answer, and it is both true and not true, and it is the only thing that has ever been true about this.
